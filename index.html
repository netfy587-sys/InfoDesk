The Symbol Garden

What Flowers and Trees Have Meant to Us, Across Cultures and Centuries

Long before botany had a formal vocabulary, plants already carried meaning — woven into myth, ritual, art, and everyday language across nearly every culture on Earth. This is a long walk through a few of the plants that carry the most.

It's easy to think of a flower as simply decorative — something pleasant to look at, chosen for color rather than meaning. But across most of human history, plants were rarely just background. They were how people marked grief and celebration, how empires signaled power, how religions built entire systems of imagery, and how ordinary conversation still quietly borrows meaning without most speakers realizing it.

A great deal of this symbolism developed independently in cultures with no contact with one another, which makes the occasional overlaps genuinely striking — and the differences even more revealing about what each culture valued, feared, or celebrated. What follows is a slow tour through six plants and the very different things they've meant, depending on where, and when, you were standing.

✦ ✦ ✦
Entry One

The Rose

Rosa

Few flowers carry as much accumulated symbolic weight as the rose, and few have meant quite so many contradictory things depending on the century. In ancient Rome, roses were scattered at both celebrations and funerals — a flower for beginnings and endings alike, hung above banquet tables as a sign that what was said "under the rose" should remain private, a custom that quietly survives in the term "sub rosa."

Ancient Rome
Secrecy, celebration, mourning
Medieval Europe
Devotion, purity, courtly love
Persian Poetry
Beauty paired with the pain of thorns

By the medieval period, the rose had become deeply entangled with religious devotion in Europe, associated closely with reverence and purity, while in Persian poetry it developed a different, more bittersweet symbolism — beauty inseparable from the thorn beside it, a recurring metaphor for love that carries both delight and pain in equal measure.

Entry Two

The Lotus

Nelumbo nucifera

The lotus holds a particularly rare distinction: it grew independently into a central spiritual symbol in two of the world's major religious traditions, Buddhism and Hinduism, without either directly borrowing the symbolism from the other. In both, the plant's unusual growth pattern — rising clean and unblemished out of muddy water — became a natural metaphor for spiritual purity emerging from difficult, imperfect circumstances.

"A flower that grows out of mud, without carrying any of the mud with it, was too fitting a metaphor for enlightenment to ignore."

In ancient Egypt, a related species carried its own separate meaning, tied to the sun and rebirth — its flowers close at night and reopen with the morning light, a daily cycle that fit naturally into Egyptian ideas about death, renewal, and the journey of the sun itself.

✦ ✦ ✦
Entry Three

The Oak

Quercus

Where flowers often symbolize beauty or fleeting emotion, trees tend to symbolize something more permanent — and few trees carry more weight in this regard than the oak. Across several unrelated European traditions, from Celtic to Norse to Greek, the oak was strongly associated with sky and thunder deities, likely because oaks are disproportionately struck by lightning compared to other tree species, a real physical pattern that ancient observers noticed and wove directly into myth.

Norse Tradition
Sacred to Thor, god of thunder
Greek Tradition
Sacred to Zeus at the Oracle of Dodona
Celtic Tradition
Strength, endurance, wisdom

Beyond its ties to thunder, the oak's sheer longevity — individual trees routinely living several centuries — made it a natural symbol of endurance and steadfastness in cultures that otherwise had little else in common, a rare case of very different mythologies arriving at strikingly similar conclusions.

✦ ✦ ✦
Entry Four

The Olive Branch

Olea europaea

The olive branch's association with peace is old enough that its exact origin is genuinely uncertain, though the story of a dove returning to Noah's ark carrying an olive leaf is one of the most widely cited sources for the symbol's persistence in Western culture. What's clearer is how thoroughly the association took hold — the olive branch appears on flags, emblems, and diplomatic seals across dozens of countries with otherwise unrelated cultural histories.

In ancient Greece, the symbolism extended beyond peace into victory and honor — olive wreaths were awarded to winners of the original Olympic Games, made specifically from wild olive branches cut, according to tradition, with a golden sickle. The tree itself was considered a gift from Athena, tightly bound into the civic identity of Athens.

✦ ✦ ✦
Entry Five

The Chrysanthemum

Chrysanthemum

Few flowers illustrate how differently the same plant can be read across cultures as clearly as the chrysanthemum. In Japan, it's a symbol of the highest honor, tied directly to the Imperial family — the Chrysanthemum Throne remains the formal name for the seat of the Japanese monarchy, and the flower's sixteen-petal form appears as the imperial seal itself.

In much of Europe, by contrast, the same flower carries a very different weight — closely associated with funerals and grief, commonly placed on graves and used in memorial arrangements. A single flower, carrying both imperial celebration and mourning, depending entirely on which side of the world it's being given.

✦ ✦ ✦
Entry Six

The Orchid

Orchidaceae

The orchid's symbolism has shifted noticeably over time, which makes it a slightly different case from the others here — less a story of cultural divergence and more a story of changing meaning within a single tradition. In ancient Greece, the orchid was tied to virility and fertility, a connection reflected in the plant's very name, derived from the Greek word for a particular root shape.

Ancient Greece
Virility, fertility
Victorian England
Luxury, rare refinement
Chinese Tradition
Integrity, quiet nobility

By the Victorian era in England, orchid cultivation had become a fashionable, expensive obsession among the wealthy — difficult to grow and import, which shifted the flower's meaning toward rarity and refined luxury. In Chinese tradition, meanwhile, the orchid took on a more restrained meaning, prized as a symbol of quiet integrity and modest nobility, often praised in classical poetry precisely for growing beautifully without seeking attention.

Why This Symbolism Still Quietly Persists

It would be easy to assume this kind of symbolism belongs entirely to the past — a poetic habit from eras before science explained pollination and plant biology in plain mechanical terms. But the symbolism hasn't actually disappeared; it's simply become so embedded in ordinary language and custom that it rarely gets noticed as symbolism at all.

A funeral still tends to involve white flowers rather than red ones, without most people consciously deciding why. A wedding bouquet still leans on roses more than any statistical analysis of flower durability would justify. An olive branch is still the universal shorthand for a peace offering, even among people who have never seen an actual olive tree.

None of this symbolism was arbitrary when it formed — it grew out of real, observed qualities of each plant: how it grows, when it blooms, what it resembles, how long it lasts. The meanings have simply outlived the original observations, carried forward mostly by habit and culture rather than active belief. That's arguably what makes a symbol durable in the first place: not that everyone still consciously believes in it, but that everyone still, quietly, keeps using it anyway.
